# 4. Discussion

## 4.1 Why use a sequential battery?

The behavioural test battery envisioned by our laboratory and assessed in this work is much more than placing three assays end to end. When the same fish undergoes the light–dark test, the novel tank test, and finally the swimming-resistance assay, every behavioural endpoint in that sequence belongs to the same animal. Questions about relationships among endpoints, which can only be approached indirectly when separate cohorts are used, therefore become directly accessible.

We designed the sequence deliberately. The light–dark test came first because we wanted to protect its approach–avoidance measurements from the potential accumulation of handling and physical disturbance. Light–dark behaviour is sensitive to experimental conditions and previous stressor exposure (Maximino et al., 2010), although this precaution should not be mistaken for an established hierarchy between the LDT and NTT, which show broadly comparable sensitivity to anxiety-like states (Kysil et al., 2017). The novel tank test followed, and the physically demanding swimming-resistance assay was placed last. Since a fish entering a later station frees the preceding apparatus, the workflow can overlap: with one animal at each station, all three assays can theoretically operate in parallel.

The practical advantages are substantial. Traditional three-domain profiling requires a dedicated cohort for each assay, whereas the battery brings all three domains together within one. When comparable sample sizes are required for each domain, this means using approximately one-third as many animals and potentially reducing animal demand and its associated workload by two-thirds. The additional comparator cohorts used here, bringing the analysed sample to 410 fish, were needed because this was a methodological evaluation; they would not ordinarily be required when the established battery is used. Fewer cohorts in turn means fewer animals purchased or bred, less aquarium capacity and maintenance, lower consumption of water, food, and other resources, less laboratory space, fewer experimental sessions, and less physical labour, along with less exposure to the psychoemotional burden of handling and euthanising animals. We did not measure that human implication directly, but it follows from requiring fewer fish and should not be overlooked. Alongside these operational and ethical gains lies a scientific one: less dependence on comparisons between separate cohorts, and broader behavioural-domain coverage from each animal.

The question, then, was not whether the battery was convenient. It was whether convenience, animal reduction, and behavioural integration came at an unacceptable scientific cost.

## 4.2 What remained stable, and where did context matter?

Our results support the continued use of the sequential design. Across 117 treatment-by-exposure comparisons derived from the 13 battery endpoints, 98 (83.8%) showed no detectable context difference after Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment. This included 73 of the 81 LDT/NTT comparisons and 25 of the 36 resistance comparisons. Differences were detected in the remaining 19 comparisons and, where Hedges' *g* was available, they were substantial relative to within-cell variability, with a median absolute *g* of 1.16.

The comparison itself deserves a word, because the two behavioural assays exchanged positions between contexts. An assay administered first has no preceding behavioural test and is therefore isolated with respect to prior behavioural testing; an assay administered second is sequential. The light–dark test was isolated in BT1 and sequential in BT2, while the novel tank test was isolated in BT2 and sequential in BT1. Each behavioural assay was thus observed in both positions. Swimming resistance was different: it was sequential in BT1 and, in BT3, isolated as an entire testing session rather than merely by position. Because separate fish were tested in each context, all of these are between-group comparisons.

This distribution matters. Sequential testing did not produce a generic or pervasive degradation of measurement. Most comparisons gave no statistical indication that placing an assay within the sequence changed its endpoint under the tested condition. The differences that did appear were selective: they accumulated in particular measurements and treatment-by-exposure combinations rather than spreading indiscriminately across the battery. That selectivity makes the interference identifiable and scientifically tractable. It does not establish formal equivalence, but neither does it support a pervasive battery effect, and it gives no reason to reject the design under the conditions tested here.

Selective context sensitivity is not unique to our design. In mice, previous testing altered particular light–dark, spatial-memory, and motor measurements while leaving others comparatively unaffected (McIlwain et al., 2001), and changing the interval between tests affected selected responses without uniform disruption (Paylor et al., 2006). Zebrafish studies come closer still: Song et al. (2016) found little change in wild-type behaviour after reversing NTT and LDT order, while Fontana et al. (2022) reported comparatively stable drug-free behaviour but treatment responses that depended on assay timing, order, and the measurement examined. Battery-context sensitivity is therefore better understood as measurement-, condition-, and perturbation-dependent than as an unavoidable consequence of testing animals more than once. Our results extend that picture beyond two anxiety-related assays, and locate the greatest concentration of context differences somewhere we did not expect.

## 4.3 The unexpected resistance result

That finding ran against our expectation. We placed the physically demanding resistance assay at the end of the sequence because we assumed that, if accumulated handling and activity imposed a cost anywhere, it would become visible there. Fish arriving after two previous assays might reasonably have been expected to perform less strongly against an increasing current.

They actually did not. In the affected conditions, the swimming-resistance index and final flow step were lower in the isolated resistance context than in the sequential battery, while the number of attempts was higher in isolation. Fish that had completed the LDT and NTT performed better against the current, not worse. Resistance became the clearest expression of measurement context in the battery, but its direction did not support fatigue. It pointed instead towards facilitation.

Our leading interpretation is that the preceding assays primed the fish for resistance. Transfer, exploration, and previous swimming may have raised arousal, motor readiness, or responsiveness to the flow stimulus. Facilitation of a later motor task by previous behavioural testing is not without precedent. McIlwain et al. (2001) found that mice that had been tested on preceding assays performed better on the rotarod than test-naive animals, and identified the rotarod explicitly as sensitive to test history. Swimming history can also affect later zebrafish performance: Little et al. (2024) reported a training effect across repeated conventional critical-swimming-speed trials, which a shortened protocol avoided. These studies do not demonstrate that the LDT and NTT activated our fish, but they show that previous testing can become incorporated into later motor performance rather than invariably impairing it.

We present the activation account as our leading explanation because it matches the observed direction, not as a demonstrated mechanism. The design cannot separate activation from elapsed time, handling, exposure timing, apparatus entry, cohort composition, or experimental batch, and arousal was not measured directly. One further observation is worth recording but not over-reading: the between-context resistance differences were not distributed evenly across exposure conditions, and the pattern is consistent with attenuation under the more severe exposures. Whether pharmacological impairment offsets the contribution of previous activity is a hypothesis requiring direct testing, not something the present comparisons can establish. These alternatives do not make the result meaningless. They tell us exactly what the next experiment needs to separate.

Swimming resistance deserves this attention because it integrates several properties of the animal at once: motor capacity, energetic state, motivation, physiological condition, and the ability to respond to the flow stimulus. Incremental swimming performance varies with body size, energetic allocation, and environmental conditions (Plaut, 2001), and zebrafish performance against a current is sensitive to fish size, flow intensity, and ethanol exposure (Blazina et al., 2013). Yet it is not simply unstable: Conradsen et al. (2016) found critical swimming speed significantly repeatable in a large zebrafish cohort measured at approximately six and nine months of age, despite contributions from sex, age, and morphology, and Little et al. (2024) reported substantial within-individual repeatability under both conventional and shortened protocols. Resistance can therefore carry a persistent individual component while still incorporating what happened to the animal beforehand, and that combination is scientifically valuable rather than inconvenient. It does not justify removing the assay; it identifies it as the one most urgently requiring methodological characterisation.

## 4.4 What the endpoints reveal together

This is where the battery earns much of its scientific value. Because every endpoint came from the same fish, we could move beyond asking what happened in each assay and begin asking what kind of behavioural profile each animal expressed across the entire sequence.

The correlation matrix showed the direct relationships among all 15 registered endpoints, while the PCA provided a broader map of their shared variation. Blood glucose remained in its own endpoint model and in the pairwise-complete correlation matrix but was excluded from the PCA because blood collection proved difficult in fish of this size with the apparatus available to us: twenty-five measurements were incomplete, and requiring glucose would have reduced the cohort from 135 to 110 fish. Setting that variable aside within this one visualisation preserved almost the entire behavioural cohort. It was a practical decision about sample preservation, not a judgement that glucose is biologically uninformative. The first two components of the resulting 14-endpoint PCA represented 40.6% of the total variance.

The clearest LDT relationship connected bright-zone occupancy with mean time per crossing (ρ ≈ 0.77) and, more moderately, with choosing the bright compartment first (ρ ≈ 0.44). The association with mean time per crossing is partly mathematical, since that measurement contains bright-zone time in its calculation; the two are related descriptions of light–dark behaviour, not independent confirmation of the same phenomenon.

More revealing was what bright-zone occupancy did *not* predict. Fish that spent more time in the illuminated compartment did not generally travel farther or swim faster in the novel tank (ρ ≈ −0.02 and 0.06), nor did they perform better against the current: occupancy was modestly associated with a lower resistance index, a lower final flow step, and more attempts to respond to the flow (ρ ≈ −0.24, −0.24, and 0.23 respectively). These associations are too weak to make bright occupancy a marker of motor impairment, but they establish something important. Fish holding the bright zone were not simply "more active fish". Occupancy describes how behaviour was allocated within the LDT, and it carries no promise about locomotor or physical performance elsewhere.

Other relationships clarify what each assay contributes. Resistance performance was inversely associated with the number of attempts (ρ ≈ −0.37): fish that performed less strongly against the current generally required more reinsertions. Zone transitions related more closely to total distance than to bright-zone occupancy (ρ ≈ 0.30 versus 0.17), although both associations were weak, so transitions carry a locomotor contribution and are not a pure measure of phototaxis. Bright-zone occupancy was not meaningfully related to upper-stratum exploration (ρ ≈ 0.09), showing that these two forms of spatial behaviour are not interchangeable and that the LDT and NTT do not reproduce a single transferable anxiety score. Condition factor was only weakly related to resistance performance (ρ ≈ 0.14), and blood glucose did not form a simple behavioural gradient across the battery.

The PCA described the same structure in relational terms: one major direction of variation joined novel-tank locomotion with resistance performance, while a second separated bright-zone occupancy and locomotor expression from the endurance measurements. These are covariance patterns among standardised endpoints, not named psychological entities, and no separation between treatment or exposure groups is inferred from them; an interactive three-dimensional version of the same analysis is available as an optional extension.

Redundancy is equally informative. The resistance index and final flow step were almost perfectly correlated (ρ ≈ 0.98), and total distance and mean velocity were strongly related (ρ ≈ 0.87); neither pair can be presented as independent confirmation. Their overlap does not make them useless, and the same qualification applies to the novel tank test as a whole: some of its locomotor information is available elsewhere, but it also captures unrestricted movement, novelty-related exploration, and vertical occupation that neither the LDT nor the resistance assay replaces. A shortened battery may eventually be possible, but no assay should be removed until the information lost with it has been evaluated directly. The strength of the battery is not that every endpoint is independent. It is that the battery reveals which measurements converge, which remain distinct, and how those relationships form a fuller behavioural profile of each fish.

## 4.5 Ethanol, order, and the stability of interference

Ethanol was not the central question of this study. Its role was to disturb the system in a biologically meaningful way and reveal whether the battery could still detect change across its domains, and it did exactly that. Ten of the fourteen modelled endpoints showed at least one adjusted treatment, exposure, interaction, or coefficient-level term under their governing models, with detected responses in the LDT, the NTT, the swimming-resistance assay, and post-test blood glucose. The battery therefore retained pharmacological sensitivity across every domain it contained rather than producing a profile dominated by a single test.

Reading those responses together exposed a behavioural transition that would have been difficult to recognise from separate cohorts. After 96 h, fish exposed to 1% ethanol occupied the bright compartment for longer while travelling less, swimming more slowly in the novel tank, and performing markedly worse against the current. Had the LDT been used alone, that increase in bright occupancy might have been reported simply as reduced avoidance or anxiolysis. Read alongside locomotion and endurance from the same animals, it becomes something less tidy and more informative: reduced avoidance may coexist with disinhibition, altered decision-making, impaired coordination, or diminished capacity under physical demand. Locomotor measurements do not reliably predict anxiety-related responses across zebrafish assays (Johnson et al., 2023), and ethanol can reduce swimming against a current or disrupt turning without an equivalent reduction in ordinary tank movement (Blazina et al., 2013). The battery does not identify which mechanism is responsible, but it prevents one endpoint from carrying an interpretation it cannot support alone.

The novel-tank results made the effect of exposure duration especially clear. At 1 h, the 1% group travelled farther and swam faster than controls; at 96 h, the same concentration produced lower distance and velocity. Direction-changing locomotor responses to ethanol are well documented in zebrafish, both across concentrations (Gerlai et al., 2000) and within acute exposure, where 20 min of 1% ethanol produced anxiolytic-like effects while 60 min impaired performance (Rosemberg et al., 2012); comparisons between acute and more prolonged exposure show a similar capacity for reversal (Mathur and Guo, 2011; Tran and Gerlai, 2013). Dopaminergic, opioid, and inhibitory GABAergic mechanisms have been proposed to contribute, but none were measured here and none can be assigned from behaviour alone. What the battery shows is the functional profile: early stimulation gave way to reduced unrestricted locomotion and weaker performance under increasing physical demand.

The resistance index and final flow step converged strongly on that endurance impairment, but their near-complete correlation means they constitute one line of evidence rather than two. Mean time per crossing requires similar caution, since it is derived partly from bright-zone time. The battery becomes more informative when these dependencies are recognised, not when every available measurement is counted as separate support.

Not all ethanol responses followed a simple direction. Upper-stratum preference showed an omnibus treatment term that adjusted pairwise comparisons did not localise, so no directional claim is made from it. Blood glucose responded through a treatment-by-exposure interaction: the adjusted contrasts distinguished the same groups at 24 and 96 h, while the raw medians reversed their arrangement between those durations, and substantial missingness reduced precision. These findings show physiological and behavioural responsiveness, but they do not support one monotonic account of ethanol across the battery. Ethanol did not act as a single switch. Its expression depended on concentration, duration, assay demands, and the measurement used to observe it.

That context differences appeared in particular treatment-by-exposure conditions raises the question that now becomes central: is the interference associated with assay position stable? It may be a reproducible feature of a measurement taken at a particular point in the sequence; its magnitude or direction may instead depend on the perturbation, so that a substance acting through another mechanism produces a different shift; or some of the variation may reflect elapsed time, handling, cohort, or batch. The present comparisons locate the interference but cannot separate these possibilities. Testing additional compounds would therefore not merely repeat the ethanol experiment, but determine whether the same shifts recur and whether previous testing interacts with pharmacological action. Fontana et al. (2022) approached this neighbouring question through counterbalanced NTT and LDT testing with delay controls, finding drug-free behaviour comparatively stable while responses to fluoxetine and conspecific alarm substance varied with assay timing and order. Their study and ours were developed independently and at approximately the same time, yet converge on the same conclusion: order and timing are experimentally tractable, treated animals may respond to them differently from untreated animals, and behavioural batteries deserve continued use and continued investigation at once.

## 4.6 From continued use to calibration

This is where the study becomes an invitation rather than a final verdict. The battery does not need every sequential measurement to reproduce its isolated counterpart perfectly before it can be useful. It needs us to understand where context enters the measurement, whether that influence is reproducible, and how it should be interpreted.

An assay performed inside a fixed sequence is not context-free, and neither is one performed in isolation. Each represents behaviour under a defined set of preceding events, handling conditions, delays, and environmental demands. If a position-dependent shift proves stable, bounded, and reproducible, it can become a known characteristic of the battery rather than an unknown source of doubt. A characterised context effect is not the same scientific problem as an uncontrolled one.

We use *calibration* to describe that process of characterisation, not an arithmetic correction. Calibration asks whether a context-related shift is reproducible, stable in direction, bounded in magnitude, dependent on assay position or on treatment, and predictable enough to be incorporated into interpretation. Answering those questions would require a consistently reported protocol tested across independent batches, untreated fish, and substances with different mechanisms. Counterbalanced-order groups, isolated-assay groups, and controls matched for handling and elapsed time would help separate assay position from previous activity, transfer, exposure duration, and cohort variation, with the statistical objective of estimating each context shift with confidence intervals.

Resistance is the obvious starting point. Future experiments could compare resistance performed first, last, and in isolation while keeping exposure duration, handling, and elapsed time as similar as possible. If fish repeatedly perform better after completing the LDT and NTT, that increase becomes a predictable property of resistance measured within the battery, and sequential values should be interpreted against data obtained under the same sequence rather than against an isolated expectation. If the shift changes with treatment or substance, previous testing and pharmacological action interact, and that interaction becomes part of the biological result. What calibration must not become is an attempt to force sequential values to match isolated ones by subtracting the present differences as correction factors. That would replace understanding with arithmetic.

Formal equivalence comes later and answers a narrower question. A non-significant difference does not demonstrate that sequential and comparison-context measurements are interchangeable, but formal equivalence is not a prerequisite for the battery to remain scientifically useful. A future confirmatory study would define in advance the largest difference considered unimportant for each selected endpoint, justifying those smallest effect sizes of interest from biological importance, measurement reliability, or the intended use of the assay rather than copying them from the differences observed here, and would test those predefined margins through two one-sided tests and confidence intervals in an appropriately powered design (Lakens et al., 2018).

The present study supplies the starting material for that programme: the measurements that were comparatively stable, those that were context-sensitive, the conditions under which differences appeared, their initial magnitude, and resistance as the principal target. Calibration is not a punishment for discovering interference. It is the advantage of knowing where the interference lives. The battery can continue to be used while that knowledge becomes sharper.

## 4.7 Limitations

The study has important limits, but they are limits on the reach of our conclusions rather than reasons to flatten the findings. Different fish were used in the three testing contexts, and assay order was fixed within each battery. The detected differences therefore represent measurement-context differences and cannot be attributed exclusively to order, previous activity, handling, elapsed time, or cohort variation. This restricts our explanation of why the differences occurred; it does not remove the observation that they were selective and concentrated.

Per-cell sample sizes ranged from 12 to 16 fish, and several resistance estimates had wide confidence intervals. Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment limited reliance on isolated findings, while effect sizes and intervals made the magnitude and uncertainty of each comparison visible; this estimation-based approach is preferable to treating significance as the only measure of importance (Calin-Jageman and Cumming, 2019), but replication with larger samples is needed to establish how consistently the resistance differences reproduce.

Ethanol concentrations were nominal because analytical verification was not available in the study records, so the pharmacological conclusions refer to administered rather than confirmed internal exposure. Resistance was measured after fish entered culture water rather than during continuous exposure; because the same transition occurred in both resistance contexts it does not explain their difference, but it does mean that resistance represents post-exposure performance. Obtaining sufficient blood from fish of this size was technically difficult, producing 25 missing glucose measurements and reducing precision for the physiological analysis. Body size and condition were recorded but not included in the cell-level context models, so morphological differences between cohorts cannot be excluded as contributors to resistance. Randomisation, scorer blinding, and the number of experimental batches were not documented in the available records, which limits assessment of these potential biases without demonstrating that the procedures were absent.

The PCA combined standardised continuous, binary, count, bounded, and ordinal measurements, including some operationally related endpoints, and was therefore used as an exploratory map of covariation rather than an inferential test or a definition of latent constructs. Finally, equivalence margins were not specified prospectively, so the absence of a detected adjusted difference cannot establish interchangeability between contexts. These boundaries restrict causal attribution, precision, and equivalence claims, but they do not reverse the central empirical pattern: context sensitivity was selective rather than pervasive, and was concentrated most clearly in swimming resistance.

## 4.8 Conclusion

Our results support the continued use of sequential multidomain behavioural testing in zebrafish. The battery generated complementary measurements of approach–avoidance, exploration and locomotion, swimming resistance, and physiology from the same animals, and when all three behavioural domains are required at comparable sample sizes it can obtain them from one cohort rather than three.

Most of the 117 evaluated treatment-by-exposure comparisons showed no detectable context difference after Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment. The differences that remained were selective rather than pervasive and accumulated particularly in swimming resistance. Their direction was also unexpected: fish tested sequentially performed better against the current in the affected conditions, opposing the fatigue effect we had anticipated. This finding does not undermine the battery. It identifies its most sensitive point and gives future methodological work a concrete target.

Ethanol produced concentration- and duration-dependent responses across every assay domain, demonstrating that the battery retained sensitivity to pharmacological perturbation. The integrated profile also showed why measuring several domains in the same fish matters: increased bright-zone occupancy could be considered alongside unrestricted locomotion and endurance, preventing one behavioural measurement from being interpreted in isolation from the animal that produced it.

The next step is to determine whether the resistance shift remains stable across assay orders, untreated fish, additional substances, exposure periods, and experimental batches. If reproducible, it can become a recognised property of resistance within the sequence; if treatment-dependent, it becomes an interaction that must be interpreted explicitly. Formal equivalence remains a later confirmatory question requiring predefined margins and adequate power.

The battery does not emerge from this study as a finished or context-free instrument. It emerges as something more useful: a practical, information-rich, animal-saving design whose strengths are clear and whose principal sensitivity can now be investigated directly. It should continue to be used, tested, and refined.
