# 1. Introduction

The zebrafish (*Danio rerio*) has travelled a long way from the developmental biology and genetics laboratories where it first became established. Over the past two decades it has grown into a mainstay of neuroscience, behavioural pharmacology, toxicology, and neuropsychiatric research, with both larval and adult fish used to study brain function and its genetic and pharmacological modulation (Kalueff et al., 2014; Stewart et al., 2014). Several features made that expansion possible. Zebrafish are small, cost-efficient, easy to breed, and can be housed in large numbers in a relatively modest space, making designs feasible at a scale that would be prohibitive in mammals (Kalueff et al., 2014). Their genome is well characterised, many strains are available, and they combine rapid development with a comparatively long lifespan. As vertebrates they share conserved neuroanatomical organisation and neurochemical systems with mammals, and the close parallels between mammalian and zebrafish behavioural paradigms suggest that many behaviours are themselves evolutionarily conserved (Kalueff et al., 2014). Crucially for pharmacology and toxicology, water-soluble compounds can be given simply by immersion, requiring neither injection nor gavage. On this foundation the field has built a substantial repertoire of standardised adult assays covering exploration, approach-avoidance, locomotion, social behaviour, learning, and physical performance (Stewart et al., 2012). It is this combination, a genuinely rich adult behavioural repertoire in an animal tractable enough to test many times over, that makes zebrafish so well suited to profiling behaviour across several domains at once.

That richness carries an interpretive challenge. Behaviour is not a single quantity, and any one assay captures only selected expressions of it. When a fish hesitates at the edge of a lit compartment, swims in short bursts near the bottom of an unfamiliar tank, or holds position against a current, it is expressing different things, and each of those things is captured by a different recorded measurement. Throughout this paper we use *endpoint* to mean one such recorded measurement, such as the seconds spent in the illuminated half of an arena or the total distance swum, and *domain* to mean the broader behavioural or physiological dimension that a set of endpoints is intended to sample. The distinction matters because an endpoint is evidence about a state, not the state itself. Treating a single measurement as though it directly represented the animal's internal condition risks overinterpretation. The practical remedy is to measure several complementary things and look for convergence among them, which is an argument for sampling more than one domain in the first place.

That argument is not new, and it was worked out most thoroughly elsewhere. The behavioural test battery, in which the same animals pass through several complementary assays, became established particularly in rodent phenotyping because researchers needed broader profiles than any single test could provide. Rodent battery studies also made the methodological costs visible: a prior test history can change later performance, this sensitivity is selective rather than universal, and even the interval between assays can matter (McIlwain et al., 2001; Paylor et al., 2006). Zebrafish research has followed a somewhat different path. Many individual assays have been carefully developed and pharmacologically characterised, and paradigms originally designed for rodents have been deliberately translated to this species (Champagne et al., 2010). Yet the same-animal, multidomain battery, and in particular the question of how measurement context shapes what such batteries record, remains less extensively characterised in fish than the individual assays themselves (Song et al., 2016; Vossen et al., 2022; Fontana et al., 2022). What is needed is a zebrafish battery that combines complementary domains while asking explicitly whether placing an assay in sequence changes what it measures.

Three assays formed the core of our design, each sampling a different domain. The light-dark test exploits the tendency of zebrafish to prefer dark over illuminated space, and records how an animal distributes its time and movement between the two compartments; it is a well-characterised assay of approach-avoidance behaviour and has been validated pharmacologically (Maximino et al., 2010; Magno et al., 2015). The novel tank test places a fish alone in an unfamiliar vertical arena, where it typically remains near the bottom before gradually exploring upward, and it yields both locomotor endpoints and measures of vertical distribution (Egan et al., 2009; Kysil et al., 2017). The swimming-resistance assay is different in kind: the animal faces a stepwise increasing current and the endpoints record how long and how well it holds position, integrating motor capacity, motivation, and energetic state in a way that no purely exploratory assay does. Assays of this general family, in which flow is raised in increments until the animal can no longer maintain position, are long established in fish physiology (Plaut, 2001), and comparable swimming-against-current measures in zebrafish are sensitive to pharmacological state and to body size (Blazina et al., 2013). We are deliberate about the language: none of these endpoints is a pure measure of anxiety. Avoidance, exploration, locomotion, motor capacity, endurance, and physiological state are distinguishable, and locomotor variables such as swimming velocity and immobility do not reliably track anxiety-like endpoints across paradigms (Johnson et al., 2023). Where an anxiety-related reading is appropriate we say *anxiety-like behaviour* and mean exactly that. Two further measurements were taken after testing: blood glucose, as a post-test physiological indicator, and Fulton's condition factor, as a descriptive index of body condition. Neither is a behavioural endpoint, and we treat them accordingly.

A behavioural battery is a planned sequence of complementary assays administered to the same animals, and it is more than several assays placed end to end. Its principal scientific gain is that the measurements belong to one individual. If a fish's approach-avoidance behaviour, its exploratory locomotion, its endurance, and its post-test physiology are all recorded from that same animal, the relationships among those domains become directly observable, rather than being inferred by comparing group averages across separate cohorts that never shared a fish. That is a qualitative change in what the data can answer, not merely a saving. The savings, however, are real and follow from the same architecture. When three domains would otherwise require comparable dedicated cohorts, one sequential cohort can in principle replace three assay-specific cohorts once the battery has been adequately characterised, with corresponding reductions in the number of animals bred, housed, handled, and euthanised, in aquarium demand, in experimental time, and in the physical labour of running the work. It also removes a source of noise: differences between cohorts cannot contaminate a comparison made within the same animals. Multidomain behavioural profiling of this kind is an active area of method development in zebrafish (Vossen et al., 2022), and its ethical and logistical appeal is straightforward. We none the less place the scientific argument first, because efficiency alone is not a reason to accept a measurement whose meaning has changed.

That caveat is the problem this study addresses. When an assay is run second or third in a sequence, the behaviour it records may reflect more than the process the assay was designed to sample. It may also carry the animal's recent history: the handling and transfers it has undergone, the novelty it has already resolved, the time elapsed since exposure to a treatment, its arousal, its accumulated physical exertion, and whatever it has learned or habituated to along the way. We refer to the whole of this as *measurement context*, in preference to narrower terms such as order effect, because a fixed sequence bundles several of these influences together and does not by itself separate them. The rodent evidence indicates that such effects are selective rather than universally disruptive (McIlwain et al., 2001; Paylor et al., 2006), and in zebrafish a novel tank and light-dark battery left baseline behaviour comparatively unchanged (Song et al., 2016), which is encouraging but cannot be assumed to extend to other domains. We built the battery in the order light-dark test, then novel tank test, then swimming resistance. The light-dark assay was placed first as a precaution, to protect it from accumulated handling and physical demand, and the physically demanding resistance assay was placed last for the same reason in reverse. We do not claim that the light-dark assay is established as intrinsically more order-sensitive than the novel tank test; the ordering was a design decision made under uncertainty, and testing that uncertainty is part of what this study set out to do.

The closest study to ours is that of Fontana et al. (2022), and the relationship between the two projects deserves to be stated plainly. They tested the novel tank and light-dark assays in the same zebrafish, counterbalanced the order of the two assays, and included delay-only control groups so that elapsed time could be separated from prior testing. In drug-free animals they found behaviour comparatively stable across testing order, with a strong correlation between performance on the two assays; under pharmacological manipulation, using fluoxetine and a conspecific alarm substance, the effects varied with test timing and order. Our study and theirs were conceived independently and at approximately the same time, and this manuscript is not an intentional extension or replication of their work; the convergence is what makes the comparison informative. What their design could not address is what remained open for us. It was not known whether a same-fish battery could be extended to include a physically demanding endurance assay, whether context effects would stay localised across a broader and more heterogeneous set of endpoints, whether such a battery would retain sensitivity to a pharmacological perturbation across all of its domains, or what relationships would become visible once behaviour, endurance, and physiology were measured in the same individuals. To address these questions we compared the full sequential battery against two comparison contexts: a reversed behavioural context, in which the novel tank assay was administered first and the light-dark assay second, and an isolated endurance context, in which the swimming-resistance assay was administered alone.

Methodological primacy does not make ethanol incidental. We needed a perturbation that would test whether the battery still registers a pharmacological signal across all of its domains, and ethanol is unusually well suited to that role for reasons that are biological as much as practical. Waterborne exposure is operationally straightforward and avoids injection. More importantly, ethanol is not a single-target drug: it acts across several conserved neurochemical systems at once, potentiating inhibitory GABA-A receptor function much as benzodiazepines do while also acting at glutamatergic NMDA receptors, the principal excitatory system (Krystal and Tabakoff, 2002). In zebrafish, dopaminergic, serotonergic, and adenosinergic signalling have all been implicated in its behavioural effects (Clayman et al., 2021), systems that contribute to arousal, reward-related behaviour, exploration, and locomotion. Because these actions are distributed rather than focal, the resulting phenotype is not fixed. Lower and higher concentrations can act in opposing directions, with stimulation and disinhibition at one end and motor impairment and sedation at the other (Gerlai et al., 2000; Dlugos and Rabin, 2003), and acute and prolonged exposures can produce different or reversed responses (Mathur and Guo, 2011; Tran and Gerlai, 2013; Rosemberg et al., 2012). This heterogeneity is exactly what makes ethanol informative here. A fish may show altered occupancy of the bright zone without a corresponding change in locomotion, or altered locomotion alongside changed endurance, and a battery that samples several domains at once is the only way to see such dissociations rather than average over them.

How context comparisons are interpreted requires one further distinction. A conventional difference test asks whether the data provide evidence that two contexts differ; failing to detect a difference is not the same as showing that the two are close enough to be used interchangeably. Demonstrating similarity is a separate exercise, requiring a smallest effect size of interest, or equivalence margin, justified in advance and an analysis designed for that question (Lakens et al., 2018). No such margins were defined prospectively here, so we frame our comparisons as estimation, reporting the magnitude and uncertainty of any context difference rather than reducing it to a binary verdict (Calin-Jageman and Cumming, 2019), and treat formal equivalence as prospective confirmatory work.

Because every endpoint came from the same fish in the sequential battery, we could also examine how the measurements relate to one another, using a rank-correlation matrix and a principal component analysis of the registered endpoints. Both analyses are exploratory and descriptive: they are not used to diagnose latent constructs or to test treatment effects, but to show which measurements converge, which remain distinct, and which are partly redundant. This study therefore had three aims, in order of priority. Our primary aim was methodological: to determine whether measurements obtained within the sequential battery differed from those obtained in the corresponding comparison contexts, and to estimate the magnitude and localisation of any context differences that were detected. Our integrative aim was to examine how endpoints measured in the same fish relate across behavioural, endurance, physiological, and condition domains. Our secondary aim was pharmacological: to determine whether the sequential battery retained responsiveness to ethanol concentration and exposure duration across its domains. We expected sequential testing to remain workable for most measurements, and we expected that any interference would prove localised to particular endpoints and conditions rather than pervasive across the battery. Whether that expectation held, and where it did not, is the substance of what follows.
